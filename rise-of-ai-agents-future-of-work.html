The Rise of AI Agents: Is Artificial Intelligence About to Change How We Work Forever?

An in-depth technology feature from The Daily Compass

Artificial intelligence is moving beyond answering questions and generating content. A new generation of AI systems is being developed to plan tasks, use digital tools and carry out multiple steps with varying levels of independence. But how much work can we really trust machines to do?

For years, most people experienced artificial intelligence through chatbots and other tools that responded to individual instructions. You could ask a question, request a summary, generate an image or draft an email. The technology produced an answer, and you decided what happened next.

Now, another development is attracting attention across the technology industry: AI agents. These systems are designed to do more than generate responses. Depending on their design and permissions, they can use tools, make decisions about the next step and work through a sequence of actions to pursue a goal.

Their development could influence how companies operate, how software is created and how people approach everyday work. Understanding this shift requires looking beyond the excitement and examining what the technology can actually do, where it struggles and what its growth could mean for society.

What exactly is an AI agent?

An AI agent is a software system designed to work towards a goal by taking actions based on information it receives and the tools available to it.

A conventional chatbot generally responds to a prompt. An agentic system may be able to break a larger task into smaller steps, choose an appropriate tool, examine the result and decide what to do next.

Imagine asking an AI assistant to help organise a business meeting. A basic chatbot might suggest an agenda and draft an invitation. An appropriately connected AI agent could potentially check approved calendars, identify available times, prepare the invitation and organise supporting documents.

These actions would depend on access to the relevant applications, the permissions granted and the system's ability to complete each step reliably. An AI agent is not automatically capable of doing everything a person can do.

How are AI agents different from ordinary chatbots?

The distinction is mainly about how a system interacts with its environment.

A chatbot is primarily designed to communicate with a user. An AI agent can be designed to interact with other software and services as part of completing a task.

This may involve three broad activities:

  • Planning: Breaking a goal into smaller actions.
  • Using tools: Interacting with approved software, databases or services.
  • Reviewing results: Checking what happened and deciding whether another step is needed.

These capabilities can be combined in different ways. Some agents operate with considerable human supervision, while others are designed to complete more steps independently. The amount of autonomy is a design choice, not a guarantee of intelligence or reliability.

Why are technology companies investing in AI agents?

Businesses are interested in AI agents because many workplace processes involve repetitive activities spread across several applications.

Employees may spend time collecting information, updating records, preparing documents, responding to routine enquiries and coordinating tasks. If an AI system can reliably assist with parts of these processes, organisations may be able to reduce delays and redirect human attention towards more complicated work.

McKinsey's Technology Trends Outlook 2026 identifies agentic software development as one of the emerging technology trends it examines. The report also discusses how AI is influencing software development, infrastructure, robotics and scientific research.

This wider development matters. AI agents are not simply another type of chatbot. They are part of an effort to connect increasingly capable AI models with the software and infrastructure used in the real world.

How AI agents could transform different industries

The potential applications extend well beyond the technology industry. However, the level of practical adoption varies considerably between sectors.

1. Customer service

AI agents could help businesses handle routine enquiries, retrieve order information, explain basic policies and direct complicated cases to human employees.

This could make customer support more responsive, but companies would still need clear ways for customers to reach a person when an issue requires judgement, empathy or special attention.

2. Healthcare administration

Administrative work in healthcare includes scheduling appointments, organising records and coordinating information. Carefully controlled AI systems could assist with some of these activities.

This does not mean that AI agents should independently diagnose patients or make important treatment decisions. Healthcare requires professional expertise, privacy protections and accountability.

3. Finance and banking

Financial organisations may explore AI agents for document processing, internal research, customer support and monitoring certain routine workflows.

Because financial decisions can have serious consequences, automated systems need strong security, human oversight and clear approval procedures.

4. Software development

AI tools can already assist developers with generating code, explaining unfamiliar functions, identifying possible errors and creating software tests.

Agentic development systems aim to coordinate more of the process, potentially helping with several stages of a software project. Human developers remain important for reviewing code, designing systems and making decisions about quality and security.

5. Research and education

AI agents could help researchers organise documents, compare information and prepare initial summaries. In education, they may support personalised learning activities and administrative tasks.

Their usefulness will depend on the quality of their sources and whether people can check the accuracy of their output. An automated research summary should never be treated as proof that every claim is correct.

Are businesses actually using AI agents?

The interest in AI agents is substantial, but enthusiasm should not be confused with widespread deployment.

Deloitte's Tech Trends 2026 report describes a gap between experimentation and operational adoption. It reports that 11% of organisations surveyed had AI agents in production, while 38% were piloting them.

These figures suggest that many organisations are still testing how agentic systems fit into their operations rather than relying on them across their entire businesses.

That distinction is important. A successful demonstration does not automatically prove that a system can operate reliably at scale, handle unexpected situations or deliver measurable business value.

Will AI agents replace human workers?

Employment is one of the most important questions surrounding this technology.

Some workplace tasks are structured and repetitive. Others involve complex judgement, negotiation, physical skills, creativity, empathy and responsibility. AI agents may be more suitable for assisting with certain tasks than replacing entire occupations.

For example, an agent might prepare a first draft of a report or collect information for a customer enquiry. A person may still need to verify the information, understand the context and approve the final decision.

This means the effect on employment could vary across industries and professions. Some roles may change significantly, some tasks may become automated, and new responsibilities may emerge.

It is too early to make confident claims about exactly how many jobs AI agents will eliminate or create. The outcome will depend on technological progress, business decisions, regulation and how workers adapt.

The risks behind autonomous AI

Giving software the ability to take action introduces risks that go beyond receiving an inaccurate chatbot response.

Errors can become real actions

An agent that misunderstands an instruction could potentially send an incorrect message, update a record or perform an unwanted action if it has the necessary permissions.

Access creates security concerns

Some agents need access to email, files, customer information or business applications. Poorly managed access could expose sensitive data or allow unauthorised actions.

Human oversight remains essential

An AI system may complete a task without fully understanding its consequences. Important financial, legal, medical and business decisions should have appropriate human review and accountability.

Reliability is not guaranteed

Agents can misunderstand instructions, encounter unexpected situations or fail when connected tools behave differently from what they expect. Organisations need ways to monitor, test and stop systems when necessary.

Why AI infrastructure matters just as much as the software

Behind advanced AI systems is a physical infrastructure of computer chips, data centres, electricity, cooling systems and communication networks.

As organisations experiment with more AI applications, demand for computing resources becomes increasingly important. Developing powerful software is only one part of the challenge; companies also need the hardware and energy required to run it.

McKinsey's 2026 technology outlook highlights the growing importance of AI infrastructure, semiconductors, energy technologies and the physical systems supporting digital innovation.

This means the future of AI agents is connected to a much larger global industry involving chip manufacturers, cloud providers, energy companies and infrastructure developers.

What should businesses and workers do now?

The emergence of AI agents does not mean every organisation should immediately automate its operations. A more sensible approach is to understand where the technology can provide genuine value.

Businesses can begin by identifying repetitive, well-defined tasks that consume considerable time. They can then test whether an AI agent can assist with those tasks under controlled conditions.

Responsible adoption should include:

  • Limiting access to sensitive information.
  • Requiring approval before important actions.
  • Checking the accuracy of completed work.
  • Keeping records of automated actions and decisions.
  • Giving employees appropriate training and oversight.

Workers can also begin learning how AI tools operate, what tasks they can support and where human judgement remains necessary.

The bigger question: What happens when software can take action?

AI agents challenge an assumption that has shaped much of the digital world: people operate software by opening applications, entering information, selecting options and coordinating different tools.

More autonomous systems could change that relationship. Instead of manually coordinating every step, people may increasingly describe an outcome and allow software to handle parts of the process.

That possibility is exciting, but it also raises difficult questions about responsibility, trust, employment and control.

The technology is developing, but its long-term impact is not predetermined. Organisations that benefit most may be those that understand where automation is appropriate, where human judgement remains essential and how to combine the two responsibly.

Final thoughts

The rise of AI agents represents an important shift in the development of artificial intelligence. The focus is expanding from systems that generate answers towards systems that can use tools and carry out tasks.

Their potential is significant, but so are the challenges involving reliability, security, accountability and employment. The difference between a promising demonstration and a dependable system will matter enormously.

The next chapter of artificial intelligence may not be defined only by how intelligently machines can answer us, but by how responsibly they can act on our behalf.
