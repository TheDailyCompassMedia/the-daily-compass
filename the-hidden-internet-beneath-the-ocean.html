The Hidden Internet Beneath the Ocean: How Undersea Cables Connect the World

A practical guide from The Daily Compass

Think about how often you use the internet. You might send a message to someone in another country, make an international payment, join a video meeting, watch a film or upload photographs to cloud storage. These activities can feel almost instantaneous.

Much of the technology behind these everyday experiences is invisible. While wireless signals connect your phone to a nearby mobile network or Wi-Fi router, enormous amounts of international digital information travel through something far less obvious: fibre-optic cables laid beneath the world's oceans.

These cables connect countries and continents, carrying information between telecommunications networks, data centres and internet infrastructure. Without them, the modern digital economy would face serious limitations.

But how can a cable sitting thousands of metres below the ocean transmit messages, financial transactions and video calls? And what happens when one breaks?

Understanding these hidden connections reveals just how much of the modern world depends on infrastructure most people never see.

What are undersea internet cables?

Undersea internet cables, also called submarine or subsea telecommunications cables, are specialised fibre-optic cables installed beneath seas and oceans to carry digital information between different parts of the world.

They contain extremely thin strands of glass or other optical materials. Instead of transmitting information as ordinary electrical signals along copper wires, fibre-optic technology sends data using pulses of light.

Those light signals represent digital information, including text, images, audio and video. At the receiving end, specialised equipment converts the optical signals back into information that computers and communication networks can process.

The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) identifies submarine cables as a fundamental part of global communications. Its overview estimates that these systems carry approximately 99% of the world's internet traffic.

This does not mean every internet connection travels underwater. Local traffic can move through terrestrial fibre, mobile networks and other infrastructure. The important point is that submarine cables carry the overwhelming majority of international data traffic.

How does information travel beneath the ocean?

The process is more sophisticated than simply placing a long cable underwater. A submarine cable system includes several connected components that work together to move information across enormous distances.

1. Fibre-optic strands carry information using light

When you send information across an international network, your data is converted into signals that can travel through optical fibre. Lasers or other optical transmitters generate pulses of light that carry the information along the cable.

Fibre-optic technology allows large quantities of information to travel over long distances with relatively low signal loss. Multiple optical channels can also carry information through the same fibre system.

2. Repeaters help signals travel long distances

Light signals gradually weaken as they travel through optical fibre. For long-distance submarine routes, equipment called repeaters is installed at intervals along many cable systems.

These devices use optical amplification technology to strengthen the signals so that information can continue travelling over long distances.

Repeaters are specially engineered for the difficult underwater environment. They must operate reliably under considerable water pressure and remain accessible for maintenance through specialised repair operations.

3. Landing stations connect the ocean cable to land

A submarine cable does not deliver internet directly to your phone. It connects to a facility on land known as a cable landing station.

At this facility, specialised equipment receives and transmits optical signals. The connection then joins terrestrial fibre networks and other telecommunications infrastructure.

From there, information can travel through internet service providers, data centres and other networks before reaching its destination.

4. Branching units connect different destinations

Some submarine cable systems are designed to serve several countries or landing locations along a route.

Branching units allow a cable system to connect to different destinations. This makes it possible for one system to serve multiple coastal locations rather than connecting only two points.

Why are these cables placed beneath the ocean?

Satellites, wireless networks and radio links also support global communications. However, fibre-optic submarine cables provide exceptionally high capacity for moving enormous amounts of data between continents.

They can carry large volumes of information with low latency and high efficiency over long distances. This makes them particularly important for international internet connections, cloud computing, financial services and other data-intensive activities.

Satellites remain important for remote locations, broadcasting, navigation and backup connectivity. They complement submarine cables rather than replacing the extensive fibre-optic network.

How the hidden cables affect everyday life

You may never see a submarine cable, but its performance can affect services you use every day.

International communication

Messaging applications, international calls, video conferencing and social media services depend on networks that exchange data across national borders.

Submarine cables help move that information between countries, allowing people, organisations and businesses to communicate across continents.

Online banking and financial transactions

International banking, card payments, financial platforms and cross-border business transactions depend on reliable communication networks.

Submarine cables help connect the systems involved in these activities. A disruption can affect the speed or reliability of some international services, depending on the networks and routes involved.

Streaming and cloud services

Watching online videos, accessing cloud-stored documents and using applications hosted in another country all involve data moving between networks.

Submarine cables provide much of the international capacity that makes these services possible.

Business and education

Businesses use international connectivity for collaboration, remote work, digital payments, customer services and access to cloud-based software.

Universities, researchers and students also rely on networks to access international learning platforms, research databases and digital resources.

What happens when an undersea cable breaks?

Submarine cables are engineered to be durable, but they are not impossible to damage.

Accidents involving fishing equipment and ships' anchors are among the major causes of cable faults. Other problems can include underwater geological activity, environmental conditions and equipment failure.

The International Cable Protection Committee reports that approximately 150 to 200 submarine telecommunications cable faults occur worldwide each year. Many are associated with accidental human activity.

A damaged cable does not automatically mean an entire country will lose internet access. Telecommunications operators may redirect traffic through alternative cables and network routes where sufficient capacity is available.

However, if several important connections are damaged at the same time, or alternative routes have limited capacity, users may experience slower connections, service interruptions or difficulties accessing certain international services.

How are damaged submarine cables repaired?

Repairing an underwater cable requires specialised ships, trained engineers and equipment designed for deep-sea operations.

First, operators investigate the fault and estimate where the cable has been damaged. They use monitoring systems and technical measurements to narrow down the location.

A repair vessel then travels to the affected area. Depending on the depth and conditions, specialised equipment is used to recover the cable from the seabed.

Engineers locate the damaged section, remove or bypass the faulty portion and join replacement cable to the existing line. The repaired section is then tested before it is returned to service.

This is not a simple operation. Weather, sea conditions, water depth, shipping activity and the availability of repair vessels can all affect how long the process takes.

Why the hidden internet matters to Nigeria and Africa

Undersea cables are particularly important to African countries seeking stronger international connectivity and more reliable digital infrastructure.

Nigeria, for example, is connected to international submarine cable systems that bring data capacity to the country's telecommunications networks.

These include systems such as ACE, Glo-1, MainOne, WACS, Equiano, 2Africa and the Nigeria-Cameroon Submarine Cable System.

Google's Equiano cable reached Lagos in April 2022 as part of a system running along Africa's western coastline. The 2Africa project has also expanded submarine cable infrastructure around the continent, with landing locations in Nigeria.

These connections can support greater international bandwidth, improve network diversity and create opportunities for digital services, businesses and data-centre development.

However, having more submarine cable capacity does not automatically guarantee affordable or reliable internet for everyone.

People still depend on terrestrial fibre networks, mobile infrastructure, electricity, internet service providers and competition within the telecommunications market. Investment across all these areas is necessary to turn international capacity into better everyday connectivity.

Are submarine cables completely safe?

No. Their location beneath the ocean protects much of the infrastructure from everyday interference, but it does not make the network invulnerable.

Accidental damage, natural events, equipment faults and deliberate interference can threaten cable systems.

There are also broader security concerns. Because submarine cables carry such important international communications, protecting landing stations, monitoring systems and the wider network is an important responsibility for governments and operators.

Network resilience depends on more than protecting one cable. Having multiple routes, diverse landing locations, effective monitoring and reliable repair arrangements can help reduce the impact of individual failures.

What does the future hold for undersea internet cables?

Global demand for digital connectivity continues to grow as more people use cloud services, artificial intelligence applications, video platforms and connected devices.

This creates demand for additional international network capacity. New submarine cable projects are being developed to connect more locations, increase capacity and provide alternative routes.

Engineers are also improving optical transmission technology, monitoring systems and cable designs. These developments can help networks carry more information and operate more reliably.

Another important development is the growing recognition that submarine cable infrastructure must be protected as critical infrastructure. International cooperation is essential because a cable route can cross several national jurisdictions.

The future of the internet will not depend on wireless technology alone. Behind faster mobile networks, connected cities and international digital services, physical infrastructure will remain essential.

The bigger picture: A connected world depends on hidden infrastructure

The internet often feels like something that exists in the air. We connect to Wi-Fi, open applications and communicate with people thousands of kilometres away without thinking about the journey our information takes.

Beneath the oceans, however, an extensive network of fibre-optic cables makes much of that international communication possible.

These systems connect economies, support businesses, enable financial transactions and help people access information across borders.

Understanding them offers a useful reminder: the digital world may be invisible on the surface, but it depends on real engineering, infrastructure and people working behind the scenes.

The next time you send a message overseas or join an international video call, remember that some of the information helping make that connection possible may be travelling through a cable resting quietly on the ocean floor.
