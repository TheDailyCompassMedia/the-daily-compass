7 Simple Ways to Protect Your Privacy on Your Smartphone

A practical guide from The Daily Compass

Your smartphone holds a lot of personal information, from private messages and photographs to banking details and saved passwords. While smartphones make everyday life easier, they can also expose your information when they are not properly protected.

The good news is that improving your phone's privacy does not have to be complicated. Here are seven simple habits that can help you protect your personal information.

1. Use a strong screen lock

Start with the first line of protection: your phone's screen lock. Use a strong passcode rather than an easy-to-guess combination such as 1234 or your date of birth. Where available, enable fingerprint or face authentication as an additional convenience.

Avoid sharing your passcode unnecessarily, and make sure your phone locks automatically after a short period of inactivity.

2. Review your app permissions

Some apps request access to your camera, microphone, contacts, location or photographs. Not every app needs all these permissions to work.

Visit your phone's privacy or app-permissions settings and review what each app can access. Remove permissions that are unnecessary. For location access, consider choosing an option such as "while using the app" when it is available and appropriate.

3. Keep your phone and apps updated

Software updates often include security fixes that address weaknesses discovered in earlier versions. Ignoring updates for long periods can leave your device exposed to known security problems.

Check for updates in your phone's settings and enable automatic updates where practical. Also update apps through the official Apple App Store or Google Play Store.

4. Be careful when using public Wi-Fi

Public Wi-Fi in places such as cafés, airports and hotels can be convenient, but you should be cautious when connecting. Confirm the correct network name with the venue instead of choosing a network simply because it looks familiar.

Avoid entering sensitive information on websites that do not use secure HTTPS connections. For banking or other important accounts, using your trusted mobile network can be a safer choice when you are unsure about a public network.

5. Turn on two-factor authentication

A password alone may not be enough to protect an important account. Two-factor authentication adds another verification step when you sign in, making it harder for someone to access your account with only your password.

Enable it for important accounts such as your email, social media and financial services. Where supported, an authenticator app or passkey can provide a useful additional security option.

6. Watch out for suspicious messages and links

Unexpected messages may claim that your account is in danger, that you have won a prize or that you must act immediately. Some are designed to trick you into revealing passwords or financial information.

Be cautious with unfamiliar links and attachments. If a message claims to come from a bank or company, visit its official website or contact it through a trusted channel instead of using the link in the message.

7. Back up important information

Protecting your privacy also means protecting your information from accidental loss. A secure backup can help you recover important photographs, contacts and documents if your phone is lost, damaged or replaced.

Use a trusted backup service, review its privacy and security settings, and protect the account with a strong password and two-factor authentication. Make sure you understand what information is being stored and where.

Final thoughts

Smartphone privacy is not about doing one complicated thing. It is about building small, consistent habits that reduce unnecessary risks. Reviewing app permissions, installing updates and protecting your accounts can make a meaningful difference.

Start with one or two changes today, then gradually work through the rest. A more secure smartphone begins with informed everyday choices.
